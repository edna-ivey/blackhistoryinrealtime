April 19 · Sports

1968 Olympic Black Power Salute

The 1968 Olympic podium protest is often remembered as a single frozen image: Tommie Smith and John Carlos, heads bowed, black-gloved fists raised. The photograph matters, but it can make the action look spontaneous or only symbolic. It was connected to months of organizing by the Olympic Project for Human Rights, to debates among Black athletes about boycotting the Games, and to a wider challenge against racism in sports and American public life. Smith and Carlos did not simply interrupt a medal ceremony. They used the most visible athletic stage in the world to insist that winning a medal did not cancel the conditions Black people faced at home.

Full Story

The medal stand in Mexico City did not become political in 1968. It already was. The Olympics claimed to rise above politics while taking place in a world shaped by colonial independence movements, the Vietnam War, apartheid, police violence, and struggles over citizenship. Black athletes knew the contradiction well. They were praised as representatives of the United States when they won, then returned to a country where housing, jobs, schools, policing, and everyday dignity were still governed by racism.

The protest grew out of the Olympic Project for Human Rights, associated most closely with sociologist Harry Edwards and a network of Black athletes and allies. Their demands included restoring Muhammad Ali's heavyweight title, removing Avery Brundage as president of the International Olympic Committee, barring apartheid South Africa and Rhodesia from the Games, and confronting racial discrimination in sports and society. Some athletes considered a boycott. Others worried that staying home would erase years of training without forcing change. The final choice was not unanimous, which is important. Movements are often remembered as cleaner than they were.

Tommie Smith and John Carlos arrived in Mexico City as elite sprinters, not as props in someone else's protest. On October 16, Smith won the 200 meters in world-record time. Carlos won bronze. Australian runner Peter Norman won silver. Before the medal ceremony, Smith and Carlos made choices about what the world would see: black socks without shoes to represent Black poverty, beads for those who had been lynched or killed, and gloves for Black unity and strength. Norman wore an Olympic Project for Human Rights badge in solidarity.

During the anthem, Smith and Carlos bowed their heads and raised their fists. The gesture lasted only a short time, but the reaction was immediate. Many reporters and officials described the act as disrespectful, militant, or improper. The International Olympic Committee, led by Brundage, pressured the U.S. Olympic Committee to punish them. Smith and Carlos were ordered to leave the Olympic Village. The same Games that could tolerate national flags and anthems treated a protest against racism as a violation of neutrality.

The backlash followed them home. They received threats. Their athletic careers suffered. Their families absorbed the pressure. They were celebrated by some Black communities and condemned by many institutions that preferred Black athletic achievement without Black political speech. Norman also paid a price in Australia, where his public support for Smith and Carlos complicated his own career and legacy.

The protest also exposed a double standard inside Olympic politics. Brundage had defended the idea that the Games should remain free of political demonstrations, but critics pointed to the Olympics' long comfort with national display, imperial symbolism, and selective enforcement. The issue was not whether politics entered the stadium. Politics had always been there. Smith and Carlos made the politics Black, visible, and impossible to manage politely.

There was also a third man on that podium whose role helps clarify the protest. Peter Norman did not raise a fist, but he wore the Olympic Project for Human Rights badge. That choice mattered because it showed solidarity did not require pretending the risks were equal. Smith and Carlos carried the central punishment, but Norman chose not to hide from the meaning of the moment.

The popular image sometimes hides the planning, risk, and disagreement behind the action. It was not only a fist in the air. It was a debate over boycott versus participation, a critique of Olympic leadership, a demand for dignity, and a challenge to the idea that Black athletes owed silent gratitude to a country that still denied Black people full safety and opportunity. The protest did not end racism in sports, but it changed the visual language of athletic dissent. Every later debate about kneeling, raised fists, warm-up shirts, armbands, and athlete speech lives in its shadow.

Cost / Impact

Smith and Carlos paid in public reputation, safety, and work. They were expelled from the Olympic Village and returned to threats, hostility, and fewer opportunities. Their families carried that backlash too. The cost was not only the loss of endorsements or races. It was the pressure of becoming a symbol before the country was ready to hear what the symbol meant.

The impact outlived the punishment. The salute made sports media confront the fiction that athletic spaces are separate from politics. It gave later athletes a visual vocabulary for protest and forced Olympic institutions to reveal which kinds of politics they considered acceptable.

Why It Matters Today

The Black Power Salute matters because it shows that protest is often condemned before it is commemorated. Today the image appears on posters, murals, documentaries, and museum walls. In 1968, the men in the image were punished. That gap between immediate backlash and later celebration is one of the most important lessons of the page.

The story also sharpens how we talk about athletes and obligation. Black athletes are often asked to represent a nation, team, school, or brand while keeping quiet about the conditions that shape their own lives. Smith and Carlos refused that split. They showed that excellence does not require silence.

A reader should leave understanding that the raised fists were not a vague gesture of anger. They were tied to demands, organizing, international politics, and a long argument over whether public stages belong only to institutions or also to the people those institutions exploit.
