March 28 · LGBTQ+ Rights & Activism

Marsha P. Johnson

Marsha P. Johnson is often placed at the front of Pride history, sometimes so quickly that the person disappears behind the symbol. She was a Black gender-nonconforming activist, performer, Christian, sex worker, friend, organizer, and survivor of police harassment, homelessness, poverty, psychiatric institutionalization, and anti-trans violence. She was present in the Stonewall era, co-founded Street Transvestite Action Revolutionaries with Sylvia Rivera, helped house young people who had nowhere safe to go, and later worked with ACT UP during the AIDS crisis. Her death in 1992 was mishandled by police and remains unresolved. A serious page has to hold both joy and danger: the flower crowns, the jokes, the generosity, the organizing, and the systems that treated her life as disposable until public memory needed a saint.

Full Story

Marsha P. Johnson was born in Elizabeth, New Jersey, in 1945, into a working-class Black family. The language available during her lifetime was not the language many readers use now. Johnson described herself at different moments as gay, a drag queen, and a transvestite, and she used she/her pronouns. The word transgender became broadly popular after her death. Respecting her means neither forcing a modern label onto every sentence nor erasing the trans history that communities now rightly claim through her life.

Johnson moved to New York City after high school with a bag of clothes and very little money. Greenwich Village offered possibility, but not safety. Police raided bars, arrested people for clothing and gender expression, and used public order laws to punish queer and trans life. Work was hard to find. Johnson survived through sex work, performance, help from friends, temporary shelter, and the informal networks built by people who had been pushed out of families, schools, churches, and jobs.

Her public style mattered. She wore bright clothes, flowers, and thrifted materials with a theatrical brilliance that refused shame. The P, she said, stood for "Pay It No Mind," a reply to people who demanded explanations of her gender. That line became famous because it was funny and sharp, but it also came from a life where refusing interrogation could be a form of self-protection.

Johnson was part of the Stonewall world, but historians are careful about the exact first moments of the uprising. Later accounts disagree about who threw what, who arrived when, and which single act began the resistance. The stronger history does not need a single heroic gesture. Johnson belonged to the street community that made Stonewall possible: people already tired of raids, humiliation, and arrests. The uprising lasted several nights because anger had been building for years.

After Stonewall, Johnson and Sylvia Rivera helped found Street Transvestite Action Revolutionaries, known as STAR. STAR did not begin as a polished nonprofit. It was emergency care by people living close to emergency themselves. Johnson and Rivera tried to house young trans and queer people, especially youth of color, who were sleeping on streets or in unsafe places. They raised money through whatever means they had, including sex work. STAR asked a direct question that some mainstream gay rights organizations avoided: what is liberation worth if the poorest people in the movement still have nowhere to sleep?

Johnson's activism continued through the 1970s and 1980s. She performed with Hot Peaches, joined protests, and remained a visible Village presence. She also experienced mental health crises, arrests, poverty, and violence. Some histories mention those hardships only to make her tragic. BHIRT has to be more careful. The hardship was real, but it did not cancel her humor, faith, friendships, style, or agency.

During the AIDS crisis, Johnson was connected to ACT UP and public health activism. In 1990 she was diagnosed with HIV. She spoke about the disease at a time when stigma, government delay, and medical neglect were killing people. Her activism sat at the intersection of Black life, queer life, trans life, poverty, public health, policing, and housing.

On July 6, 1992, Johnson's body was found in the Hudson River. Police initially ruled the death a suicide. Friends and community members objected, saying the investigation was dismissive and that anti-LGBTQ violence in the area had been serious. The case was eventually reopened in 2012 and classified as undetermined. No one has been held responsible.

Johnson is now honored in parks, murals, documentaries, and public memory. That recognition matters, but it can become too clean. The same city that now celebrates her name once criminalized the ways she survived. To remember Marsha P. Johnson honestly is to honor the beauty and also ask why it took so long for institutions to treat her life as worth investigating.

Cost / Impact

Johnson's work cost her safety, privacy, money, and physical security. She gave support to younger people while she herself lived with housing insecurity. She organized inside movements that often sidelined trans people, sex workers, poor people, and people of color. Her death exposed how easily police and media could dismiss violence against someone already treated as marginal.

Her impact is not only symbolic. STAR made housing part of queer liberation. Her life forces LGBTQ+ history to include race, class, gender expression, policing, HIV/AIDS, and survival labor. Pride without that context becomes a parade with the hardest questions removed.

Why It Matters Today

Marsha P. Johnson matters today because public memory can love a radical person after death while ignoring the conditions that shaped her life. Housing instability, violence against Black trans women, HIV stigma, police indifference, and exclusion inside movements are not past-tense issues.

She also changes how Stonewall should be taught. The uprising was not only a bar story or a single-night story. It was a street-community story, a survival story, and a story about people who had little institutional power but knew how to protect one another.

Remembering Johnson well means refusing both erasure and sainthood. She was not a perfect symbol. She was a full person who helped make freedom more concrete for people who needed food, shelter, friendship, and courage before they needed a slogan.
