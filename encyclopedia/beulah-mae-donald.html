March 11 · Justice

Beulah Mae Donald

Beulah Mae Donald did not have the kind of power institutions usually recognize. She was a grieving mother in Mobile, Alabama, after Klansmen murdered her son Michael Donald in 1981. But she made a decision that changed the legal and financial future of one of the country's most violent white supremacist organizations. With attorneys including Michael Figures and the Southern Poverty Law Center, Donald sued the United Klans of America and won a $7 million civil verdict in 1987. The case did not bring Michael back. It did something else: it made organized racial terror pay, not only in criminal court but with property, money, and institutional collapse.

Full Story

Beulah Mae Donald's story begins with a crime that white supremacists meant as a message. In March 1981, Michael Donald, a 19-year-old Black man in Mobile, Alabama, was abducted and murdered by members of the United Klans of America. His body was left hanging from a tree. The killing came after anger among Klansmen over a mistrial involving a Black defendant accused of killing a white police officer. Michael Donald was not connected to that case. He was chosen because he was Black.

The murder exposed how racial terror did not end neatly with the classic civil-rights era. This was 1981, not 1881 or 1961. The United Klans of America had been connected to earlier violence, including attacks on Freedom Riders, the murder of Viola Liuzzo, and the 16th Street Baptist Church bombing through members associated with its world. By the 1980s its power had declined, but it remained dangerous. Donald's death was a lynching in modern Alabama.

Criminal prosecutions eventually brought convictions. James "Tiger" Knowles pleaded guilty in federal court. Henry Hays was convicted of murder. But Beulah Mae Donald and her lawyers pursued a different question: could the organization itself be held responsible? That was not a symbolic question. White supremacist groups often survived by treating violence as the work of isolated members while benefiting from the fear those members created.

The civil suit, Donald v. United Klans of America, was filed in 1984. Attorneys including Michael Figures and the Southern Poverty Law Center argued that the Klan's structure, rhetoric, and authorization made the organization responsible for the acts of its members. The case relied on civil accountability as a tool against organized hate. It asked a jury to see racial terror not only as individual violence, but as institutional action.

In 1987, an all-white jury awarded Beulah Mae Donald $7 million. The amount mattered because the United Klans could not absorb it. The organization was forced to turn over its headquarters property in Tuscaloosa. The verdict effectively bankrupted the group. Encyclopedia of Alabama notes that the United Klans was officially dissolved in 1992 after the Donald case crippled it financially.

This history should be told with care. A lawsuit did not erase white supremacy. It did not repair Beulah Mae Donald's loss. It did not mean every hate group could be ended by one verdict. But it created a model: civil law could reach organizations that encouraged or enabled violence, even when criminal cases focused on individual perpetrators. That strategy would influence later cases against hate groups.

Beulah Mae Donald's role is sometimes reduced to "mother of victim," but that phrase can hide agency. She chose to continue after criminal convictions. She sat through painful legal proceedings. She allowed her grief to become a demand for institutional accountability. Forgiveness, if discussed, should never be used to soften the violence or make the story more comfortable. The public lesson is not that Black mothers are endlessly strong for other people's inspiration. The lesson is that she forced a system to assign cost to organized racial terror.

The case also changes how we talk about lynching memory. Michael Donald is often described as one of the last recorded lynching victims in the United States. That phrase is historically significant and deeply sobering, but it should not make lynching sound safely past. The systems that made his murder possible included organized white supremacy, racialized criminal accusation, and law enforcement failures. Beulah Mae Donald's victory showed that accountability had to reach beyond the men who held the rope.

Cost / Impact

The cost to Beulah Mae Donald was immeasurable. She lost her son to racial terror and then had to relive the details in court. Her family carried grief, public attention, and danger. The legal victory did not compensate for the life taken. Money and property could punish the organization, but they could not restore Michael Donald.

The impact was nonetheless historic. The verdict bankrupted the United Klans of America and helped establish a strategy for using civil suits against hate groups. It showed that organized violence could be attacked through assets, charters, property, and institutional responsibility, not only through individual criminal convictions.

Why It Matters Today

Beulah Mae Donald matters today because hate violence is organized through networks, money, rhetoric, and permission. Treating every attack as isolated can protect the institutions that make violence more likely. Donald's case pushed the law to follow the organization behind the act.

Her story also warns against turning grief into a tidy lesson. Black families have too often been asked to transform racial violence into national moral education. Donald's victory deserves respect because it was specific: a lawsuit, a verdict, a property transfer, a damaged organization. The power is in the concrete result.

A reader should leave understanding that accountability can be financial, organizational, and historical. Beulah Mae Donald made the Klan answer not only for what two men did, but for the world that taught them to do it.
