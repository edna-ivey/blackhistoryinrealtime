Ericka Huggins

Ericka Huggins is often introduced through the most dramatic parts of her life: the Black Panther Party, the killing of her husband John Huggins, the New Haven trial, and two years in jail before charges were dismissed. Those facts matter, but they do not complete the story. Huggins also became a poet, editor, educator, meditation teacher, college instructor, and director of the Oakland Community School, one of the Black Panther Party's most important survival programs. Her page matters because it moves past the old question of whether the Panthers were only confrontation. Huggins shows the other daily work: feeding children, writing curriculum, surviving state surveillance, building schools, and finding a spiritual practice strong enough to carry grief without turning away from struggle.

Full Story

Ericka Huggins's life asks readers to hold more than one truth at once. She was part of the Black Panther Party, an organization that carried guns, challenged police violence, ran survival programs, faced infiltration and prosecution, and also contained internal conflict and harm. She was a young widow, a political defendant, a mother separated from her child, and an educator who helped make a radical school feel like a place of care. Flattening that life into either danger or inspiration misses the history.

Huggins was born Ericka Jenkins in Washington, D.C., in 1948. As a teenager she attended the 1963 March on Washington. Her official biography describes that day as a moment when she committed herself to serving people. She later attended Cheyney State College and Lincoln University, where she met John Huggins, a Vietnam veteran. The two moved into the orbit of the Black Panther Party, which had been founded in Oakland in 1966 by Huey P. Newton and Bobby Seale.

The Panthers are often remembered through images of leather jackets, berets, rifles, and police standoffs. Those images are not false, but they are incomplete. The party also created free breakfast programs, health clinics, liberation schools, newspapers, sickle-cell testing, and community defense work. Huggins entered that world as a young woman whose political formation included both confrontation with state violence and the daily work of community care.

In Los Angeles, Huggins and her husband helped build Panther organizing. On January 17, 1969, John Huggins and Alprentice "Bunchy" Carter were shot and killed on the UCLA campus during conflict involving members of US Organization. Huggins had recently given birth to their daughter, Mai. The loss made her a widow and single mother almost immediately, while the political world around her kept moving.

After returning to New Haven, Connecticut, Huggins helped lead the local Panther chapter. In May 1969, she and Bobby Seale were arrested in connection with the killing of Alex Rackley, a Panther member who had been accused by some members of being an informant. The case was grim and politically charged. National Archives records summarize that police had an audio tape on which Huggins was heard interrogating Rackley, and that Huggins and Seale were tried for murder, kidnapping, and conspiracy. In 1971, after a long trial, the jury deadlocked, reportedly 10 to 2 for Huggins's acquittal, and Judge Harold Mulvey dismissed the charges against both defendants.

That paragraph cannot be treated casually. Rackley was killed. Huggins was not convicted. State surveillance and Panther internal crisis both belong in the historical frame. The New Haven case drew national "Free Bobby, Free Ericka" organizing, exposed the pressure placed on Panther chapters, and left Huggins separated from her daughter while awaiting trial. Huggins has described teaching herself meditation while incarcerated as a way to survive isolation and grief.

After release, Huggins continued in the party. She worked as a writer and editor for the Black Panther Intercommunal News Service and published poetry. Then she took on the work that would become central to her long public legacy: education. The Oakland Community School grew from earlier Panther educational experiments, including liberation schools, the Children's House, and the Intercommunal Youth Institute. By 1973, the school opened in East Oakland and served children from the broader community, not only children of party members.

Huggins directed the Oakland Community School from 1973 to 1981. In her own writings and oral-history interviews, she described a school organized around respect, food, curiosity, field trips, political education, academic seriousness, and the idea that children should learn how to think rather than what to think. The school served meals, paid attention to children's whole lives, and treated the classroom as part of community survival. Students were not abstractions in a policy debate. They arrived hungry, brilliant, tired, funny, afraid, and ready to be taken seriously.

The school also complicates the public memory of the Panthers. If the only image is armed patrol, the Oakland Community School disappears. If the only image is a loving school, the violence, surveillance, and internal contradictions disappear. Huggins's life refuses that simplification. She kept working inside a movement that was both necessary and wounded, and she later turned toward teaching, restorative justice, meditation, and healing work without denying the political world that shaped her.

Her story matters because education appears here as survival, not charity. Children needed breakfast, safety, books, art, field trips, history, and adults who believed they were capable. Huggins helped build that in a period when Black radical women were often expected to carry the labor of movements while men became the public face. Her life makes the reader ask who does the care work after the rally, after the trial, after the funeral, and after the headline leaves.

Love is a great power. Use it to transform your world.
Ericka Huggins, official website
Cost / Impact

The cost of Huggins's work was personal and political: widowhood, surveillance, public accusation, incarceration, separation from her infant daughter, and years of labor inside a movement under pressure from outside and within. Her story also includes the cost borne by Alex Rackley and his family, which responsible history cannot erase while discussing the New Haven case.

Her impact is visible in educational practice and movement memory. The Oakland Community School offered a model of community-controlled schooling that treated food, health, dignity, and intellectual challenge as connected. Huggins also carried meditation and healing into activist spaces long before those conversations became common in movement culture.

Why It Matters Today

Huggins matters today because movements still struggle to balance defense, care, discipline, accountability, and healing. Her life does not let readers imagine activism as only protest or only service. It is both, and sometimes it is harder than either word suggests.

Her work at the Oakland Community School also speaks to current debates about public education, culturally grounded curriculum, restorative practice, and community control. The question she helps raise is direct: what would schools look like if children were treated as whole human beings rather than future test scores or problems to manage?

A reader should leave this page understanding that Black radical history includes classrooms, poems, meals, grief, courtrooms, and spiritual survival. Huggins did not step away from politics when she turned toward children and healing. She widened what politics was allowed to include.
