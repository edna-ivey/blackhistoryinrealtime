March 14 · Literature & Cultural Preservation

Zora Neale Hurston

Zora Neale Hurston wrote Black life as if Black people were already enough. That sounds simple now because her influence is everywhere. In her own lifetime, it was risky. Hurston was a novelist, folklorist, anthropologist, performer, and collector of stories who treated Black Southern speech, humor, religion, work, desire, and argument as serious knowledge. She grew up in Eatonville, Florida, studied at Howard and Barnard, collected folklore in the South and Caribbean, and wrote Their Eyes Were Watching God in 1937. Some contemporaries criticized her for not writing protest fiction in the expected way. Later, her books fell out of print and her grave went unmarked until Alice Walker helped revive public attention. Her page matters because recovery is not just praise after neglect. It asks why a writer who saw Black people so clearly had to be found again.

Full Story

Hurston's childhood in Eatonville matters because it gave her a different starting point from many Black writers of her generation. Eatonville was a Black-governed town. Its porch talk, arguments, sermons, jokes, markets, politics, and ceremonies gave Hurston an early model of Black life not organized around white observation. White power still surrounded the town, but inside Eatonville, Black people governed, performed, judged, loved, fought, and narrated themselves.

Hurston left Florida and rebuilt her education as a young adult, including time at Morgan Academy in Baltimore, Howard University, and Barnard College. At Howard, she moved among Black intellectuals and artists. In New York, she entered the Harlem Renaissance with a personality that people remembered even when they disagreed with her. She was funny, theatrical, ambitious, and difficult to categorize.

At Barnard, Hurston studied anthropology with Franz Boas and worked in a field still shaped by outsiders collecting the lives of people they treated as subjects. Hurston changed the terms. She was not a detached visitor to Black Southern culture. She knew the speech, rhythms, jokes, silences, and performance traditions from within. That insider knowledge did not make her work less scholarly. It made different kinds of evidence available.

Her folklore collecting took her through Florida, Alabama, Louisiana, the Bahamas, Haiti, Jamaica, and other places. She gathered stories, songs, religious practices, work songs, hoodoo traditions, and community performances. Mules and Men, published in 1935, remains important because it preserved Black oral culture without treating it as primitive material waiting for white interpretation. Hurston understood performance as intelligence. A story could entertain and carry law, memory, warning, flirtation, theology, and history at the same time.

Their Eyes Were Watching God arrived in 1937. The novel follows Janie Crawford through marriage, labor, desire, violence, grief, and self-possession. Its language is central to its power. Hurston did not use Black vernacular as decoration. She made it the engine of thought and beauty. The book did not ask white readers to certify Black humanity. It built a world where Black speech could carry philosophy.

Some contemporaries were not persuaded. Richard Wright criticized the novel, arguing that it lacked the protest force he believed Black literature needed. Alain Locke also found limits in her work. Their criticism belonged to a real debate: What should Black art do under racist pressure? Should it expose oppression to the country? Should it build interior worlds without explaining itself? Should it document folk culture, attack power, or invent beauty? Hurston's answer was not that racism did not matter. It was that Black life was larger than the literature of injury.

Hurston's politics were not simple. She could be conservative in ways that trouble many later readers, including her opposition to Brown v. Board of Education. She also wrote with fierce independence, skepticism of patronage, and refusal to let any movement tell her exactly what Black art had to be. Taking her seriously means not sanding down those contradictions. The same writer who preserved Black Southern culture with unmatched brilliance could also take positions that many Black contemporaries and later scholars rejected.

By the 1950s, Hurston's reputation had faded. Her books were out of print. She worked different jobs, wrote where she could, and struggled financially. She died in Fort Pierce, Florida, in 1960 and was buried in an unmarked grave. The rediscovery story often centers Alice Walker, and Walker's 1975 essay was crucial. But recovery was also the work of Black feminist scholars, librarians, teachers, local historians, and readers who understood that the canon had not simply forgotten Hurston by accident. It had been shaped by gender, class, politics, publishing, and assumptions about what counted as serious Black writing.

Today Hurston is central to American literature. That return should not make her story too tidy. She was not waiting politely to become a symbol. She was a working writer who argued, performed, researched, misjudged, delighted, offended, preserved, and created. Her work survives because it was too alive to stay buried.

Sometimes, I feel discriminated against, but it does not make me angry. It merely astonishes me.
Zora Neale Hurston, "How It Feels to Be Colored Me"
Cost / Impact

Hurston paid for her independence. She lost patronage, endured criticism, struggled financially, and died without the institutional security her work deserved. Being a Black woman writer who refused a single acceptable political script made her vulnerable to neglect from white publishers, male critics, academic institutions, and parts of Black literary leadership. Her late-life poverty was not a measure of her talent. It was a measure of how easily the literary marketplace discards people after using their brilliance.

Her impact is larger than rediscovery. Hurston changed the sound of American literature. She helped preserve Black oral traditions, expanded what anthropology could hear, and gave later writers a model for Black women's interior life, humor, sexuality, and speech. The recovery of her work also helped build Black feminist literary study as a field.

Why It Matters Today

Hurston matters today because readers still argue over what Black art owes the public. Must it protest? Must it explain? Must it be respectable? Must it be legible to outsiders? Hurston's work answers by refusing the narrowness of the question. Black life contains protest, but it also contains gossip, laughter, flirtation, work, faith, grief, bad choices, and beauty. All of that is worthy of literature.

Her anthropology also matters in a time when culture is often consumed without accountability. Hurston documented Black folklore as knowledge created by communities, not raw material for outsiders to mine. She listened for the intelligence inside performance. That approach asks today's readers to value cultural forms on their own terms before translating them for someone else's comfort.

The recovery of Hurston's reputation is a warning. A writer can be essential and still be pushed out of print. A grave can go unmarked while the work remains alive in people who remember. Canon is not neutral. It is made by editors, schools, critics, money, politics, and rescue work. Hurston teaches us to ask who else is waiting in the archive because the gatekeepers were not listening.
