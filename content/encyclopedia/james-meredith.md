## Answer Text

## Subject

James Meredith and the March Against Fear

## Daily Story

Imagine deciding to walk 220 miles across Mississippi by yourself.

Not with thousands of supporters.

Not with television cameras following every step.

Alone.

That was James Meredith's plan in June 1966.

Meredith wanted to prove that a Black citizen could travel through Mississippi without fear. Four years earlier he had become the first Black student admitted to the University of Mississippi, surviving riots, threats, and constant federal protection.

Now he wanted to test whether the state had really changed.

It hadn't.

On the second day of the march, a white sniper stepped out from the trees and shot Meredith.

Most people would have quit.

Instead, while Meredith recovered in the hospital, civil rights leaders continued the march in his name. Thousands joined. When he was healthy enough to walk again, Meredith returned and completed the final miles himself.

The man who tried to stop the march only made it larger.

## Story

June 6, 1966.

James Meredith stepped onto a Mississippi highway carrying a walking stick and a mission. He wasn't leading a protest. He wasn't surrounded by reporters. He wasn't asking anyone to follow him.

He was walking alone.

Meredith called it the March Against Fear. His goal was simple: prove that a Black American could walk through Mississippi without being intimidated, threatened, or attacked. It was a test of freedom itself.

The idea came from experience.

Four years earlier, Meredith had become the first Black student admitted to the University of Mississippi. The federal courts had ruled that he had the right to attend. Many Mississippians disagreed.

When Meredith arrived on campus in 1962, riots broke out. Two people were killed. Hundreds were injured. President John F. Kennedy sent federal marshals and U.S. Army troops to restore order.

Even after enrollment, Meredith lived under constant protection. He attended classes while armed guards watched nearby.

Most people would have considered that enough history for one lifetime.

Meredith did not.

In 1966 he decided to walk from Memphis, Tennessee, to Jackson, Mississippi. He wanted to encourage Black voter registration and show that fear no longer controlled where Black citizens could go.

On the second day of the march, a white sniper named Aubrey James Norvell stepped from the roadside brush and opened fire.

Meredith was hit by shotgun pellets in the head, neck, and back.

The attack was captured by photographers.

Images of the wounded Meredith spread across the country.

Civil rights leaders including Martin Luther King Jr., Stokely Carmichael, and Floyd McKissick decided the march would continue.

Thousands joined.

What began as one man's walk became a movement.

While Meredith recovered in the hospital, the march grew larger every day. Along the route, organizers registered Black voters and held community meetings. By the time the march reached Jackson, thousands of people were participating.

Then Meredith did something remarkable.

He rejoined the march.

Still recovering from his wounds, he walked the final miles himself.

The sniper had failed.

Meredith later said the purpose of the march was never simply reaching Jackson. It was proving that fear could not determine where a citizen belonged.

That lesson remains his legacy.

## Wait... What?!

James Meredith started the March Against Fear alone on purpose.

He wanted to prove that a Black citizen could walk through Mississippi without being terrorized. On the second day, he was shot by a white sniper on the side of the road.

Then, after recovering in the hospital, Meredith came back and finished the march.

The man who tried to stop him only made the march bigger.

## Cost / Impact

Meredith paid a physical and emotional price for challenging segregation. At Ole Miss, he needed federal protection just to attend class. During the March Against Fear, he was shot in the head, neck, and back.

His courage also exposed the danger Black citizens faced when trying to vote, travel, or simply move freely. The attack brought national attention to Mississippi and helped push thousands of Black citizens to register to vote.

## Why It Matters

James Meredith understood something important: rights only matter when people can actually use them.

His life reminds us that legal victories are only the beginning. Someone must be willing to walk through the door, attend the school, cast the vote, or take the first step.

Meredith kept taking those steps even when he knew the risks.
