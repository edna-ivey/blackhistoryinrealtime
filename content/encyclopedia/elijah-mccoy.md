## Subject

Elijah McCoy and the Invention of Automatic Lubrication

## Daily Story

The railroad ran on steam, and steam engines needed oil. But in 1866, you had to stop the train to oil the engine — every eighty miles or so, the whole machine halted while workers climbed underneath and lubricated the moving parts by hand. Trains waited. Schedules broke. Money was lost.

Elijah McCoy had a better idea.

He had grown up in Canada, the son of parents who escaped slavery in Kentucky through the Underground Railroad. He trained as a mechanical engineer in Edinburgh, Scotland. Then he came to Michigan looking for engineering work — and found that no railroad would hire a Black engineer.

So they gave him a fireman's job: shoveling coal and oiling engines by hand.

While he was doing the work the system said was all he deserved, he was thinking about how to fix the problem.

In 1872, McCoy patented his first automatic lubricator — a device that fed oil to engine components through a system of tubes while the train was still moving. No more stops. No more delays. Just continuous movement and a machine that ran better.

Railroad engineers learned quickly: McCoy's lubricator worked better than every imitation. They started demanding "the real McCoy" before accepting a shipment.

That phrase is still used today to mean the genuine article, not a substitute. Most people using it have no idea where it came from.

## Story

George Elijah McCoy was born on May 2, 1844, in Colchester, Ontario, Canada. His parents, George and Emillia McCoy, had escaped slavery in Kentucky by way of the Underground Railroad. Their son was born in freedom — which gave him access to education that would have been illegal for him in the United States.

His parents recognized that Elijah had mechanical gifts and sent him to Edinburgh, Scotland, for an apprenticeship in mechanical engineering. He spent years learning to design, build, and repair machines at a professional level. He earned real credentials in a serious technical field.

Then he came back to North America to work.

In the United States after the Civil War, a Black man with a mechanical engineering degree could not find work as a mechanical engineer. The Michigan Central Railroad offered him a position — as a fireman. That meant shoveling coal into the furnace that powered the steam engine, and oiling the engine's moving parts every time the train stopped.

McCoy took the job and went to work. He was overqualified for what he was doing, and he knew it. But he paid attention.

Steam engines had a fundamental problem. The metal parts that moved against each other required constant lubrication to prevent overheating and damage. The only way to apply that oil was to stop the machine. For a railroad, stopping the machine meant stopping the train — roughly every eighty miles, the train halted, workers went underneath, and everything got hand-oiled before the train could move again.

McCoy watched this process hundreds of times. He understood the mechanics of the engine better than anyone around him. And while he was down there oiling the parts by hand, he was working on a solution.

In 1872, he filed his first patent: a self-acting lubricator for steam engines. The device fed oil through a series of tubes to the engine's moving components using steam pressure, automatically and continuously, while the train was in motion. The lubrication happened without stopping. The engine ran better and lasted longer. The railroad saved time and money on every trip.

McCoy refined the design repeatedly over the following years, filing patent after patent as he improved how the oil was delivered, how the flow was regulated, and how the mechanism held up under different operating conditions. Competing manufacturers tried to copy his devices. The copies didn't work as well. Engineers on the railroads learned to tell the difference, and they began refusing substitute lubricators — demanding, specifically, the genuine McCoy product before they would accept delivery.

The phrase "the real McCoy" entered the language. It still means the authentic article, not an imitation. Most people who use it have never heard his name.

McCoy went on to receive 57 patents across his lifetime, covering lubricating devices for different kinds of machinery including ship engines and factory equipment. The principles he established — continuous automatic lubrication of moving parts — became foundational to industrial engineering well beyond railroads. Modern lubrication systems in cars, aircraft, and heavy machinery descend from the concepts he developed in a Michigan workshop in the 1870s.

In 1920, McCoy founded the Elijah McCoy Manufacturing Company in Detroit to produce his inventions directly. The company struggled financially despite the value of his patents — a pattern familiar to Black inventors of the era who held the ideas but were often cut out of the profits by manufacturers and investors who held capital.

He died on October 10, 1929, in Eloise, Michigan, at 85. At the time of his death, he held 57 patents and was largely unknown outside engineering circles.

The man who kept America's railroads running could not get hired as an engineer. He invented his way around it.

## Wait... What?!

Elijah McCoy trained as a mechanical engineer in Scotland. He came to America with credentials the railroad industry refused to recognize because he was Black.

So they gave him a job oiling engines by hand.

He used that job to study exactly what the machines needed — and invented a device that lubricated them automatically while they were still moving. His invention worked so much better than the imitations that engineers began demanding "the real McCoy" before accepting any substitute.

That phrase outlasted him. Most people who use it today have no idea they are quoting the name of a Black inventor whose skills his employers refused to acknowledge — a man who built his laboratory out of the job they said was all he deserved.

## Cost / Impact

McCoy held 57 patents by the time he died, but he earned recognition long after the inventions had already made other people wealthy. Patent ownership and manufacturing profit were two different things, and McCoy spent his career on the inventor's side of that divide. The Elijah McCoy Manufacturing Company he founded in 1920 struggled financially despite the value of what he had built.

The impact of his work extended far beyond the railroad industry. Automatic lubrication principles he developed were adapted for factory machinery, marine engines, and eventually automotive systems. The ability to keep machines running without stopping for maintenance was one of the core enablers of continuous industrial production in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. McCoy's 1872 patent addressed a real problem that was limiting American industry, and he solved it in a machine shop while working as a fireman.

The State of Michigan and city of Detroit eventually honored him with historical recognition. His name, preserved in everyday language, is used millions of times a day by people who have never heard of him.

## Why It Matters

McCoy's story is the story of Black innovation under constraint. He had the credentials, the training, and the talent to work as an engineer. The system refused him. Instead of leaving the field, he turned the job they gave him into the research he needed to solve a problem the industry hadn't solved. His inventions shaped American industrial production for generations. The phrase he inspired remains in everyday use. And the name attached to it — the real McCoy — is his.
