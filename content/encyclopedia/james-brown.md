## Answer Text

"Say It Loud — I'm Black and I'm Proud," released in August 1968, was a deliberate political statement at a time when calling yourself "Black" — rather than "Negro" or "colored" — was itself a declaration. The song reached number one on the R&B charts despite resistance from radio stations.

## Subject

James Brown and the Sound of Black Pride

## Daily Story

The night Martin Luther King Jr. was shot — April 4, 1968 — cities across America started burning.

James Brown was scheduled to perform in Boston the next day. City officials asked him to cancel the show. He said no. He would perform, but he wanted it televised live so people could watch from home instead of taking to the streets.

The mayor agreed. The governor agreed.

That night, while riots broke out in over 100 American cities, Boston mostly stayed quiet. Historians and city officials have credited the broadcast — and Brown's presence — as a factor that kept the city calm.

This was not an accident. James Brown had been thinking about the relationship between music and Black political life his entire career.

He had come from extreme poverty in South Carolina, dropped out of school, shined shoes and danced for pennies, and served time in a reform school as a teenager. He got out through music. Then he spent thirty years building an entertainment empire with his own record label, his own publishing company, and his own touring operation — traveling up to 330 nights a year.

In 1968, while the country was burning, he made a record called "Say It Loud — I'm Black and I'm Proud." Some radio stations refused to play it.

It became a hit anyway.

Brown never separated music from politics. He understood that saying something out loud — in a song, to a crowd, over a television broadcast — was itself a political act.

## Story

James Joseph Brown Jr. was born on May 3, 1933, in Barnwell, South Carolina. His early life was shaped by poverty severe enough that he was sometimes turned away from school for not having adequate clothing. His parents separated when he was young, and he was raised partly by relatives in Augusta, Georgia. He ran numbers for local gamblers. He shined shoes. He danced for pennies outside Augusta's radio station.

At 16, he was arrested for breaking into cars and sentenced to the Alto Reform School in Toccoa, Georgia. At the reform school, he formed a gospel group and met Bobby Byrd, who would become his earliest musical partner. When Brown was released in 1952, Byrd's family took him in. The Famous Flames followed.

In 1956, the group recorded "Please Please Please" for the Federal label. It sold more than a million copies. James Brown was 23 years old.

What happened next was unlike anything in American music history.

Brown didn't simply make hit records — he reinvented how popular music was structured. He stripped everything down to rhythm. He pushed the guitar, bass, and horns into percussive roles, treating them as rhythm instruments rather than melody carriers. He built his sound on the downbeat — the first beat of the bar, where most popular music at the time stayed light. The result was something new: funk.

In 1962, he recorded *Live at the Apollo*, a live concert album his label didn't want to release because they didn't believe live records sold. Brown paid for the recording himself. The album reached number two on the Billboard pop charts and spent 66 weeks on the chart. It remains one of the most important live albums in music history, and it was released over the label's objections because Brown believed in it.

He kept working at a pace that stunned the industry. He performed up to 330 nights a year. He was meticulous about his band — he fined musicians for missed notes and wrong notes during performances. He controlled everything about his show. He became known as the hardest-working man in show business, a nickname he had earned.

Then came 1968.

Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated on April 4. Within hours, cities began burning — Washington, Chicago, Baltimore, Louisville, Kansas City. Boston was on edge. James Brown was scheduled to perform there the following night at Boston Garden.

City officials asked him to cancel.

Brown negotiated. He would perform, but he wanted the concert broadcast live on local television so that people could watch from home. Mayor Kevin White agreed and went on television himself to encourage people to stay home and watch. The broadcast reached an audience far larger than the Garden could hold.

Boston remained largely calm that night while riots spread across the country. The decision to broadcast the concert — and Brown's willingness to perform rather than cancel — is remembered as a significant factor.

Later that same year, Brown released "Say It Loud — I'm Black and I'm Proud." The song was a deliberate political choice. At a time when "Black" was still a contested word — when mainstream media still defaulted to "Negro" or "colored" — Brown put "Black" in the title, recorded it with a choir of children repeating the chorus, and released it at the height of the Black Power movement. Some radio stations refused to play it. It reached number one on the R&B charts anyway.

James Brown died on December 25, 2006, in Atlanta. He was 73 years old. His recordings are among the most sampled in music history. Every major genre of Black popular music that came after him — funk, hip-hop, R&B — carries his structural innovations in its DNA.

He came from almost nothing. He built everything he had. And he used what he built to say, as loudly as he possibly could, that Black people had every reason to be proud.

## Wait... What?!

The night after Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated, James Brown performed a live concert in Boston — but had it televised so people would stay home and watch rather than go into the streets.

Cities across America were burning.

Boston didn't.

Brown used music as a deliberate tool to hold a city together on one of the most volatile nights in modern American history. He made a strategic decision to perform rather than cancel, broadcast rather than contain, and trust the audience rather than fear them.

It worked. And he knew what he was doing.

## Cost / Impact

Brown grew up in poverty severe enough that he was turned away from school for lacking adequate clothing. He served time in a reform school at 16. Everything he built, he built without a safety net — and once he built it, he used it.

He invested his platform deliberately. The Boston concert the night after King's assassination was not a routine performance — it was a calculated use of his public trust at an extraordinary moment. "Say It Loud — I'm Black and I'm Proud" was recorded during a period when Black self-identification was politically charged, and radio stations that refused to play it were making a political statement by their refusal.

The musical impact is structural. Brown's rhythmic innovations — the emphasis on the downbeat, the use of horns and guitars as percussion, the layered polyrhythmic patterns — defined what popular music sounds like in America. Hip-hop, which emerged in the 1970s, built itself on James Brown samples. Hundreds of the most sampled recordings in music history are his. His influence is not historical — it is present in nearly every genre of contemporary American music made today.

He structured Black pride into the music itself so that anyone who danced to it was participating in it.

## Why It Matters

Brown demonstrated that cultural production was political action. His 1968 concert in Boston showed that a performer with genuine community trust could hold a city together on a night when others were burning. His recordings gave language and rhythm to Black pride at a moment when both were needed. The innovations he invented in the 1960s are still the structural foundation of American popular music, heard in every song that follows the patterns he built.
