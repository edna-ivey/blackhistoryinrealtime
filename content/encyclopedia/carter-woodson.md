## Subject

Carter G. Woodson and the Creation of Negro History Week

## Daily Story

Here's what they didn't teach you: Black history wasn't left out of textbooks by accident. It was left out on purpose.

Carter G. Woodson figured that out the hard way. He grew up poor in Virginia, taught himself to read as a kid, and eventually earned a PhD from Harvard — one of only two Black Americans who had ever done that. Then he opened a history textbook. And he couldn't find himself or his people in it.

So he decided to fix it himself.

In 1926, Woodson created *Negro History Week* — a single week in February, timed to the birthdays of Frederick Douglass and Abraham Lincoln, because he knew schools were already talking about those two men. His plan: get Black history into a conversation that was already happening, then expand from there.

It worked. Schools started teaching it. Communities started celebrating it. And over the next fifty years, that one week grew into something nobody could ignore.

In 1976 — fifty years after Woodson started it — the United States officially recognized Black History Month.

He didn't live to see it. But he built everything it stands on.

## Story

Picture a twelve-year-old in Virginia who can barely go to school — his family needs him in the fields. He works long days. He watches the world move around him. And in whatever time he has left, he teaches himself to read. That kid grew up to earn a PhD from Harvard University — one of only two Black Americans to do so at the time. His name was Carter G. Woodson, and that degree was just the beginning.

Carter Godwin Woodson was born on December 19, 1875, in New Canton, Virginia, the son of formerly enslaved parents. Money was always tight, and formal schooling came very late — he didn't enroll in high school until he was 20 years old. But once he got through the door, he didn't slow down. He earned his PhD from Harvard in 1912, becoming only the second Black person in history to reach that milestone, after W.E.B. Du Bois. He had worked harder than almost anyone to earn those three letters behind his name.

Then Woodson picked up an American history textbook. And he barely found himself in it — or anyone who looked like him. Black people had built this country, survived centuries of violence, fought in every war, and changed everyday life with their inventions and ideas. But the textbooks acted like none of that had ever happened. Woodson realized something that would reshape the rest of his life: if the system wasn't going to tell this story, he was going to have to build a whole new one. Not just write one book. Build the entire infrastructure, brick by brick.

In 1915, he co-founded the Association for the Study of Negro Life and History — now known as ASALH — and the following year launched the *Journal of Negro History*, a peer-reviewed publication that treated Black history with the scholarly seriousness it had always deserved. Then came 1926. Picture Woodson at his desk with a calendar, choosing the second week of February because it fell near the birthdays of both Frederick Douglass and Abraham Lincoln. He called it Negro History Week, printed materials, and sent them to schools, churches, and community centers across the country, asking people to teach it. Thousands responded. The week spread from city to city, school to school.

In 1933, Woodson published *The Mis-Education of the Negro*, a book arguing that American schools had trained Black students to undervalue their own history and culture. It was a bold, uncomfortable argument — and the book is still in print today. Woodson died on April 3, 1950, never seeing his week become a month. Negro History Week was officially expanded to Black History Month in 1976, when President Gerald Ford recognized it nationally. Woodson had been gone for 26 years. But everything he built was still standing.

## Why It Matters

Without Woodson's deliberate institutional work, Black history would have remained absent from school curricula and public consciousness for generations longer. He didn't wait for the system to change — he built the infrastructure himself: the association, the journal, the annual observance. Every February, that infrastructure operates, whether or not his name is attached to it.
