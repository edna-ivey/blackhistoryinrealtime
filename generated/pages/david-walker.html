July 5 · Resistance

David Walker

David Walker's Appeal was printed in 1829, but it sounds like someone refusing to whisper in a burning room. Walker was born free in North Carolina to a free mother and enslaved father, moved to Boston, joined Black organizing networks, sold used clothing, worked with Freedom's Journal, and wrote one of the boldest antislavery pamphlets in the United States. Southern authorities feared it because it named slavery as sin, theft, hypocrisy, and violence, and because copies reached Black readers through sailors, clothing networks, and oral reading. Walker matters because he refused the polite timetable of white reform. He wrote as if Black freedom was urgent because it was.

Full Story

David Walker wrote like a man who had no interest in making enslavers comfortable. His Appeal to the Coloured Citizens of the World, first published in 1829, attacked slavery, racism, colonization, white Christianity, and the hypocrisy of a republic that praised liberty while holding Black people in bondage. It was not a petition begging white America to become moral. It was a warning, a sermon, a history lesson, and a call to Black people to recognize their own dignity.

Walker was born free in Wilmington, North Carolina, around 1796. His mother was free and his father was enslaved. That family line placed freedom and bondage inside the same household story. He grew up in a slave society even though he was legally free, which meant he saw how racial power shaped movement, labor, law, and violence. He later traveled and eventually settled in Boston, where free Black institutions offered both refuge and political energy.

Boston's Black community mattered. Walker lived around Beacon Hill, joined the Methodist May Street Church, participated in Prince Hall Masonry, and became active in the Massachusetts General Colored Association. He also sold used clothing, a business that placed him in contact with sailors and travelers. That kind of network mattered for print. Pamphlets could move in coat pockets, trunks, ships, and conversations.

His work also belonged to a Black public sphere that was building itself under pressure. Freedom's Journal, mutual-aid groups, churches, lodges, conventions, and reading circles gave Black people ways to argue with each other and with the nation. Walker's shop was part of that world. It was commerce, but it was also contact, conversation, and political distribution in a period when slaveholding states treated Black knowledge as a threat.

The Appeal drew from the Bible, classical references, the Declaration of Independence, antislavery argument, and Black political anger. Walker directly challenged Thomas Jefferson's racist claims and rejected colonization schemes that proposed sending free Black people to Africa. He insisted that Black people belonged in the United States because their labor, suffering, and claims were already woven into the country.

One reason the pamphlet terrified Southern authorities was that Walker addressed Black readers as political actors. He did not present enslaved people as passive victims waiting for benevolent reform. He wrote to them as people capable of judgment, memory, courage, and resistance. The pamphlet was meant to be read aloud, which allowed its message to travel even where literacy was violently restricted.

Southern officials responded with alarm. Copies appeared in port cities, and mayors and governors tried to stop circulation. Some states strengthened laws against antislavery literature, Black sailors, and Black assembly. The panic revealed the power of print and the fear white officials had of Black communication. A pamphlet could become a security crisis because slavery depended on controlling what people could know and imagine.

Walker died in Boston in 1830, not long after publishing revised editions. Rumors of poisoning circulated because the Appeal had made him enemies, but contemporary records cited consumption, and his daughter died of the same disease around that period. Responsible history should name the speculation without turning it into fact. The truth is already stark: Walker lived under threat because he told the truth about slavery in a country trying to criminalize that truth.

His influence reached later abolitionists. William Lloyd Garrison, Frederick Douglass, Maria Stewart, Henry Highland Garnet, and others engaged a world Walker helped electrify. He did not create Black abolitionist thought alone, but his pamphlet became one of its fiercest printed monuments.

David Walker's story is about more than radical language. It is about Black print culture, maritime networks, church life, free Black organizing, and the politics of fear. The Appeal frightened enslavers because it broke the rule that Black people should wait quietly for justice. Walker refused waiting.

America is as much our country, as it is yours.
David Walker, Appeal to the Coloured Citizens of the World
Cost / Impact

Walker paid for his truth-telling with danger. His name became associated with a pamphlet Southern authorities tried to suppress. Free Black people, sailors, and readers faced increased scrutiny because white officials feared the movement of antislavery ideas. Walker's sudden death intensified the sense that radical speech could carry mortal risk, even when the recorded cause was disease.

His impact was intellectual and practical. The Appeal gave abolitionism a sharper Black voice, challenged colonization, defended Black belonging, and showed how print could become resistance. It also forced enslavers to reveal how fragile their system felt when confronted by words aimed at Black readers.

Why It Matters Today

Walker matters today because he exposes the difference between being included in a nation and claiming it. He did not ask whether Black people could someday belong. He argued that Black labor, suffering, and humanity already made belonging undeniable.

His story also shows why literacy, publishing, and distribution are political. The authorities feared not only what Walker wrote, but who might read it aloud. Ideas move through communities, and systems built on domination know that communication can become action.

A reader should leave Walker understanding that Black freedom thought has always included urgency, anger, faith, strategy, and uncompromising critique.
