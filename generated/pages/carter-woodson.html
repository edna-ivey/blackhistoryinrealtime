Full Biography

Carter Godwin Woodson was born on December 19, 1875, in New Canton, Virginia, the son of formerly enslaved parents. Unable to attend school regularly as a child due to farm labor, he taught himself to read and later enrolled in high school at age 20. He went on to earn a Ph.D. from Harvard University in 1912, becoming only the second African American to do so after W.E.B. Du Bois.

Woodson dedicated his life to correcting the systematic exclusion of Black history from American education and public memory. In 1915, he co-founded the Association for the Study of Negro Life and History (ASNLH), and the following year launched the Journal of Negro History — a peer-reviewed publication that brought scholarly rigor to African American history for the first time.

In 1926, Woodson established Negro History Week during the second week of February, timed to align with the birthdays of Frederick Douglass and Abraham Lincoln. His goal was direct: ensure that Black history was taught in schools, recognized in communities, and preserved for future generations. The observance spread steadily across the country over the following decades.

In 1933, Woodson published The Mis-Education of the Negro, a landmark critique arguing that American education had conditioned Black people to undervalue their own history and culture. The book remains in print today.

Negro History Week expanded to Black History Month in 1976 when President Gerald Ford officially recognized it nationally. Woodson, who died on April 3, 1950, is remembered as the "Father of Black History."

Those who have no record of what their forebears have accomplished lose the inspiration which comes from the teaching of biography and history.

— Carter G. Woodson

Why This Matters

Without Woodson's deliberate institutional work, Black history would have remained absent from school curricula and public consciousness for generations longer. He didn't wait for the system to change — he built the infrastructure himself: the association, the journal, the annual observance. Every February, that infrastructure operates, whether or not his name is attached to it.
