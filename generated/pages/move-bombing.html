May 13 · Justice

MOVE Bombing

On May 13, 1985, Philadelphia police dropped explosives on a rowhouse at 6221 Osage Avenue after a long confrontation with MOVE, a Black liberation and back-to-nature organization that city officials had treated as a public-order problem for years. The fire that followed killed eleven people, including five children, and destroyed more than sixty homes. The story is sometimes told as an extreme police mistake. That is too easy. The bombing was the result of official decisions: surveillance, escalation, military-style tactics, an explosive device, a command choice to let the fire burn, and years of conflict between MOVE, neighbors, police, and city government. Its page matters because it shows what happens when a city treats a Black neighborhood as expendable in the name of control.

Full Story

MOVE began in Philadelphia in the 1970s around John Africa's teachings, which rejected many modern institutions and emphasized natural living, communal life, and resistance to state authority. Members took the surname Africa, protested, used amplified speech, clashed with neighbors and officials, and drew intense police attention. The relationship between MOVE and the city grew out of real neighborhood complaints, ideological conflict, racism, policing, and official choices that repeatedly escalated rather than resolved the conflict.

The 1978 confrontation in Powelton Village hardened everything. Police attempted to evict MOVE from a house. Officer James Ramp was killed during the confrontation, and nine MOVE members were later convicted in his death. MOVE maintained that the prosecution was unjust and that police violence was being ignored. City officials and police treated MOVE as a dangerous enemy. By the early 1980s, some MOVE members were living in a rowhouse on Osage Avenue in a Black middle-class neighborhood. Neighbors complained about noise, sanitation, and threats. The city failed those neighbors too, first by allowing the situation to deteriorate, then by choosing a militarized response.

On May 13, 1985, police moved against the house. They evacuated nearby residents, surrounded the block, and attempted to force MOVE members out. Police fired massive amounts of ammunition into the house, used water cannons, and deployed tear gas. The confrontation was broadcast in real time. Then, from a state police helicopter, officers dropped an explosive device onto a bunker-like structure on the roof.

The bomb started a fire. Officials knew fire was possible, and firefighters were present. The decision that followed remains one of the most devastating parts of the record: authorities allowed the fire to burn for a period in the hope of destroying the bunker and forcing MOVE members out. Instead, the fire spread through the rowhouses. Eleven people died: six adults and five children. More than sixty homes were destroyed. A Black neighborhood was left burned and traumatized.

The official aftermath did not repair the harm. The Philadelphia Special Investigation Commission later condemned the city's actions, calling the decision to drop a bomb and let the fire burn indefensible. Mayor W. Wilson Goode appointed the commission and publicly accepted responsibility, but accountability remained limited. A grand jury did not bring criminal charges against city officials. Survivors and residents pursued civil litigation. In 1996, a federal jury found that the city had used excessive force and violated constitutional rights of survivors.

The harm continued in the built environment. Homes rebuilt on Osage Avenue were plagued by construction problems, buyouts, and years of dispute. Residents who had already lost homes to fire were forced into another long struggle over whether the city would make them whole. Memory itself became another battlefield. For decades, the bombing was treated as a local wound rather than a national lesson about policing and state violence.

The story became even more painful when later reporting and institutional review revealed mishandling of remains associated with the bombing, including remains of children used or held by institutions without family consent. Philadelphia apologized in 2021, but apology arrived after generations of damage. Apology does not return the dead, rebuild trust, or erase the sight of a city dropping explosives on its own residents.

Teaching the MOVE bombing requires refusing simple binaries. MOVE was a controversial organization with real conflict with neighbors. The city had an obligation to address public safety. But no public-safety obligation required a bomb, a fire allowed to burn, dead children, and a destroyed neighborhood. The central question is not whether the situation was difficult. It is why officials chose tactics that made a Black residential block disposable.

Dropping a bomb on an occupied row house was unconscionable.
Philadelphia Special Investigation Commission report, 1986
Cost / Impact

The cost was immediate and human: eleven people killed, five of them children, families displaced, homes destroyed, and a neighborhood changed forever. Survivors carried physical danger, grief, legal battles, and public suspicion. Neighbors who had asked the city for help lost the very homes the city claimed to protect.

The impact reaches beyond Philadelphia because the bombing exposes how official language can make extreme force seem administrative. Words like eviction, operation, barricade, and public safety can hide decisions that put entire communities in danger. The rebuilt houses, later construction failures, lawsuits, and apology show that state violence continues long after smoke clears.

Why It Matters Today

The MOVE bombing matters today because police militarization and urban governance are still live issues. Cities still decide which neighborhoods are protected, which are surveilled, which are overpoliced, and which are treated as acceptable collateral damage. Osage Avenue shows that those decisions can become catastrophic when officials value control over life.

It also matters because public memory often minimizes state violence when the victims are described as difficult, radical, poor, Black, or disruptive. A person's politics does not erase the state's responsibility. A neighborhood complaint does not justify collective punishment.

The fuller lesson is about decision chains. The bombing was not one bad second. It was a sequence of choices made by people with titles, budgets, weapons, radios, and authority. Accountability has to follow the chain, not stop at the word tragedy.
