June 20 · Justice

Fred Hampton

Fred Hampton was 21 when Chicago police killed him during a predawn raid planned with FBI involvement. That sentence is true, but it can trap him at the moment of death. Hampton was also a gifted organizer from Maywood, Illinois, a former NAACP youth leader, deputy chairman of the Illinois Black Panther Party, builder of free breakfast and health programs, and a coalition-maker who connected Black, Puerto Rican, and poor white organizers in Chicago. The state feared him not only because he was angry, but because he was effective. Hampton matters because his story is not just about repression. It is about what power recognizes as dangerous: disciplined organizing, political education, community programs, and alliances across lines that segregation and poverty were supposed to keep apart.

Full Story

Fred Hampton's story is often told backward from the raid. That is understandable because the raid was deadly and politically revealing. But starting only with December 4, 1969, risks letting the state define the frame. Hampton became a target because of what he was building before the police entered the apartment.

Hampton grew up in Maywood, west of Chicago. He was young, charismatic, and unusually skilled at making political language feel immediate. Before joining the Black Panther Party, he worked with the NAACP Youth Council, organizing around recreation, education, and community conditions. That training mattered. Hampton was not simply a speaker who appeared from nowhere. He had practice turning complaint into demands and demands into organization.

He joined the Black Panther Party in Chicago in 1968 and quickly rose to leadership as deputy chairman of the Illinois chapter. The Panthers carried a militant image, and they did believe in armed self-defense against police violence. But the Illinois chapter's daily work also included free breakfast for children, political education, legal consultation, health programs, and neighborhood organizing. Hampton's genius was making those programs feel connected to a larger analysis of power.

Chicago in 1969 was deeply segregated and governed through neighborhood inequality, machine politics, police power, and racial division. Hampton pushed against that division. He helped form alliances with the Young Lords, a Puerto Rican organization, and the Young Patriots, a group of poor white migrants from Appalachia. The coalition was later remembered as the first Rainbow Coalition. It did not pretend that race did not matter. It asked whether groups facing different forms of poverty, displacement, and police abuse could organize against shared structures while keeping their own identities and demands.

That coalition work was one reason Hampton was dangerous to authorities. A militant group isolated in one neighborhood could be contained more easily than a disciplined organizer who could speak across communities. The Chicago History Museum's encyclopedia notes that the Panthers' service programs challenged city authority and that their militant image left them open to negative press and police raids. Both things were true. Hampton's work threatened existing power because it competed for legitimacy in the lives of people city hall had failed.

The FBI's COINTELPRO program targeted the Black Panther Party nationally. National Archives records describe Hampton as the focus of FBI investigation. William O'Neal, an FBI informant inside the Chicago Panthers, supplied information about the organization. Later accounts and National Archives summaries state that O'Neal drugged Hampton on the night before the raid. The raid was carried out by Chicago police assigned to the Cook County State's Attorney's office.

Before dawn on December 4, 1969, officers entered Hampton's apartment. Mark Clark, a Panther from Peoria, was killed near the entrance. Hampton was in bed with his pregnant fiancee, Deborah Johnson, now known as Akua Njeri. Hampton was killed. Other Panthers were wounded and arrested. Authorities initially claimed there had been a fierce shootout. Later evidence undermined that account. National Archives summaries note that police fired 99 shots while Panthers fired once. Charges against surviving Panthers were eventually dropped.

The aftermath mattered because it exposed how official stories are built. Photographs, ballistics, survivor testimony, and litigation challenged the police narrative. Hampton's family and survivors filed a civil suit that dragged on for years before settlement. The case did not bring Hampton back, and it did not undo COINTELPRO. But it helped place the raid within a broader record of state repression against Black radical organizing.

Hampton's politics should not be sanitized. He was a revolutionary socialist, a Panther, and a critic of capitalism, racism, and police power. He spoke in the language of revolution because he believed reform had failed poor and Black communities. Readers do not have to agree with every part of his politics to understand why he mattered. The historical task is to take the politics seriously instead of turning him into either a threat or a martyr without ideas.

Fred Hampton's life was short, but it was not small. He organized children eating breakfast, young people studying politics, communities making alliances, and poor people identifying common enemies without erasing their differences. The raid ended his life. It did not erase the question his organizing left behind: what becomes possible when people the system keeps apart begin to recognize one another as allies?

Cost / Impact

The cost was life, grief, fear, and public distortion. Hampton was killed at 21. Mark Clark was also killed. Survivors faced arrest, injury, and years of legal struggle. Deborah Johnson survived the raid while pregnant. The community then had to fight not only the killing, but the official story told about it.

Hampton's impact survives in the language of coalition and survival programs. His work linked free breakfast, health care, legal aid, political education, and multiracial organizing into one theory of power. His death also became one of the clearest examples of COINTELPRO-era repression against Black political organizing.

Why It Matters Today

Hampton matters today because public memory often treats state violence as a moment and organizing as background. His life reverses that. The organizing came first, and the repression responded to it.

His Rainbow Coalition also matters because multiracial politics can be shallow when it ignores power, and impossible when groups never meet. Hampton's model was not colorblind unity. It was solidarity rooted in concrete needs: food, housing, health care, safety, and political education.

A reader should leave understanding why a 21-year-old organizer could frighten institutions with far more money and weapons. He was building disciplined relationships. Power knows that relationships can become infrastructure.
