June 4 · Resistance

Angela Davis

Angela Davis became world-famous because the state tried to make her disappear into a criminal case. But her story did not begin with the FBI's Ten Most Wanted list, and it did not end with acquittal. Davis is a scholar, organizer, communist, feminist, and prison abolitionist whose life connects universities, surveillance, Black liberation politics, political imprisonment, and the modern critique of the prison-industrial complex. The famous trial matters because it showed how ideas, associations, and race could be turned into public fear. The longer story matters because Davis kept thinking and organizing after the spectacle moved on.

Full Story

Angela Davis was born in Birmingham, Alabama, in a neighborhood so marked by racist bombings that Black residents knew violence as part of the landscape. She grew up in a family that valued education and political awareness. That early world matters because Davis did not become political when the FBI named her. She came from a Black Southern context where law, terror, schooling, and resistance were already connected.

Davis studied philosophy and became part of left intellectual circles in the United States and Europe. She was associated with the Communist Party USA and Black liberation politics, including work around political prisoners. At UCLA, her teaching appointment became a public fight because of her Communist Party membership. The dispute was about more than one job. It asked whether a university could claim intellectual freedom while punishing a Black woman scholar for radical politics.

In August 1970, a courthouse attack in Marin County, California, left several people dead, including Judge Harold Haley. Guns used in the attack were registered to Davis. Prosecutors charged her with crimes including murder, kidnapping, and conspiracy, arguing that she was connected to the plan. Davis went underground, was placed on the FBI's Ten Most Wanted list, and was arrested in October 1970. The state turned her into a symbol of danger before a jury heard the case.

The "Free Angela" movement became international. Supporters saw Davis as a political prisoner targeted for her ideas and associations. Her case drew attention from activists, artists, students, and political organizations around the world. The campaign also forced people to ask how race, gender, communism, and fear shaped public opinion. Davis was not only defending herself in court. She was being made to stand for a whole set of anxieties about Black radicalism.

In 1972, Davis was acquitted of all charges. The acquittal mattered because it rejected the prosecution's case, but it did not erase the machinery that had produced it: surveillance, sensational media coverage, political repression, and the ease with which a Black woman intellectual could be framed as uniquely threatening. Davis emerged from the trial with a larger platform and used it.

Her public image carried layers that the trial could not contain. The Afro, the wanted poster, the courtroom photographs, and the campaign posters made Davis instantly recognizable. But images can flatten a person. They can turn a scholar into a symbol and make the symbol easier to love or hate than the ideas. Davis kept insisting on the ideas: capitalism, racism, prisons, feminism, empire, and the ways state violence teaches people to accept cages as common sense.

The gender politics of the case also mattered. Davis was portrayed through familiar racist and sexist scripts: too angry, too dangerous, too intellectual, too connected to movements that respectable society feared. Supporters answered with a different image of Black womanhood, one that linked beauty, discipline, study, and resistance. The fight over Davis was also a fight over who had the authority to define Black women in public.

Her later work became central to prison abolition and critiques of the prison-industrial complex. Davis argued that prisons should not be treated as natural or inevitable institutions. She connected prisons to racism, capitalism, gender violence, policing, education, and abandoned social responsibilities. Her abolitionist writing asks readers to imagine safety through housing, health care, education, repair, and accountability rather than only cages.

Davis has also written and spoken about Black feminism, international solidarity, Palestine, labor, and liberation movements. Some readers encounter her first as an icon on a poster. The encyclopedia has to do more. It should show the thinker, the defendant, the teacher, the organizer, and the person whose ideas remained threatening because they asked what society uses punishment to hide.

Cost / Impact

Davis paid with surveillance, imprisonment before trial, public demonization, and the threat of life in prison. Her family, colleagues, students, and supporters also lived inside the pressure of the case. Being turned into a symbol meant losing control over how strangers imagined her.

Her impact reaches law, politics, scholarship, and organizing. The Free Angela campaign became a model of international solidarity around political trials. Her later abolitionist work helped move prison criticism from the margins into universities, community organizations, and public debate.

Why It Matters Today

Davis matters today because arguments once dismissed as too radical now shape mainstream conversations about policing, prisons, sentencing, bail, surveillance, and political repression. Agreeing with every Davis position is not required to understand her importance. The point is that she forced questions institutions preferred to avoid.

Her story also warns readers about spectacle. A mugshot, wanted poster, or headline can make guilt feel settled before evidence is tested. Davis's acquittal does not simply close the story. It asks how often public fear does legal work before a trial begins.

The deeper lesson is about ideas and danger. Davis became dangerous to the state not because she stopped thinking, but because she connected thought to organizing. Her life shows what can happen when scholarship refuses to stay safely decorative.
