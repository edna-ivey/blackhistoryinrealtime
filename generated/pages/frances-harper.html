July 6 · Literature

Frances E.W. Harper

Frances Ellen Watkins Harper was a poet, novelist, lecturer, journalist, abolitionist, suffragist, temperance organizer, and Black women's club leader. The daily challenge names Iola Leroy, but Harper's life was bigger than a title on a syllabus. Born free in Baltimore, orphaned young, and educated in a Black school run by her uncle, she became one of the nineteenth century's most widely heard Black women public speakers. She wrote poems against slavery, traveled the antislavery lecture circuit, challenged white suffragists to face racism, and helped build institutions for Black women after the Civil War. Harper matters because she refused to choose one freedom struggle at a time. She understood that race, gender, family, labor, education, and citizenship were braided together long before later generations had neat language for it.

Full Story

Frances E.W. Harper belonged to a generation of Black women who had to make public authority from materials the nation tried to deny them: education, print, travel, speech, faith, and mutual aid. She was born free in Baltimore in 1825, but free Black life in Maryland was not safety. Freedom could be hemmed in by racist law, economic vulnerability, surveillance, and the constant threat that slavery's power would reach into the lives of people who were not legally enslaved.

Harper was orphaned around age three and raised by her aunt and uncle. Her uncle, William Watkins, was an educator, minister, and abolitionist who ran the Watkins Academy for Negro Youth. That school mattered. It placed Harper inside a free Black intellectual world where reading, moral argument, and public service were treated as tools for survival. She left school as a young teenager to work, including in a white household with access to books. The archive often makes such labor sound small. For Harper, books and work became part of the same education.

She began publishing early. Forest Leaves appeared in 1845, when Harper was still a young woman. In 1854, Poems on Miscellaneous Subjects reached print with antislavery support and circulated widely. The Library of Congress copy shows a writer using poetry and prose to speak against slavery, defend Black dignity, and imagine moral responsibility as public work. Harper was not simply writing private feeling. She was writing into movements.

Maryland helped make her next step unavoidable. A law passed in the early 1850s threatened free Black people from Maryland who returned after living out of state with imprisonment and sale into slavery. Harper, who had gone north to teach, could not safely go home. That exile was political. She turned toward antislavery lecturing, speaking for groups such as the Maine Anti-Slavery Society and Pennsylvania Anti-Slavery Society.

Public lecturing by a Black woman in the 1850s required courage and stamina. Harper traveled in a country where trains, hotels, halls, and streets were structured by race and gender. She spoke before audiences who might admire her, doubt her, patronize her, or threaten her. She also wrote for antislavery papers, placing herself in Black print culture alongside figures such as Frederick Douglass and William Still. Poetry, journalism, and oratory were not separate lanes for her. They were different ways to move the same freedom argument.

Harper's fiction widened the field. In 1859, The Two Offers appeared in the Anglo-African Magazine. It is often identified as the first short story published by an African American woman. The story's concerns with women's choices, education, marriage, and moral independence show Harper thinking about gender before the Civil War made formal citizenship questions unavoidable. She did not treat Black women as side characters in the drama of freedom. She placed their decisions and constraints at the center.

After marrying Fenton Harper in 1860, she stepped partly into family life. His death in 1864 left her with a daughter to support, and Harper returned to lecturing. The Civil War and Reconstruction changed her subjects, not her central commitment. She spoke for emancipation, education, temperance, women's rights, and Black citizenship. In 1866, at the National Woman's Rights Convention, she delivered We Are All Bound Up Together. The speech challenged white suffragists who spoke about women's rights while failing to fully confront racial injustice. Harper insisted that Black women carried a double burden and that reform movements could not be honest while leaving them outside the circle of concern.

That position put her in difficult terrain. The postwar suffrage movement split over the Fifteenth Amendment, which protected voting rights regardless of race but did not enfranchise women. Harper supported Black male suffrage while continuing to demand women's rights. She understood the pain of the compromise, but she also knew how urgently Black communities needed political protection in the face of white violence. Her politics were not simple because the world she faced was not simple.

Iola Leroy, published in 1892, brought decades of thinking into fiction. The novel follows slavery, passing, family separation, education, Reconstruction, and public responsibility. It is sometimes described as sentimental, but that label can be lazy when applied to nineteenth-century Black women writers. Harper used feeling as political argument. She asked readers to care about families slavery had torn apart and to imagine Reconstruction as a moral rebuilding, not only a legal aftermath.

In her later years, Harper helped build Black women's institutions, including the National Association of Colored Women. Club work was not polite distraction. It was infrastructure: schools, homes, mutual aid, anti-lynching work, temperance campaigns, youth programs, and a public voice for Black women who were often ignored by white reformers and Black male leaders alike. Harper spent her life making the same demand in different forms: freedom must be large enough to include the people movements are tempted to overlook.

We are all bound up together
Frances E.W. Harper, National Woman's Rights Convention, 1866
Cost / Impact

Harper paid the cost of public Black womanhood in the nineteenth century: dangerous travel, economic insecurity, exclusion from white-led reform spaces, and the burden of arguing for humanity before audiences that often wanted reform without equality. Maryland law also made home unsafe, turning her political work into a life shaped by displacement.

Her impact moved through books, newspapers, lecture halls, suffrage meetings, temperance unions, and Black women's clubs. She helped create a tradition of Black feminist public thought before that phrase existed, insisting that antislavery, women's rights, education, labor, and family freedom belonged in the same conversation.

Why It Matters Today

Harper matters today because movements still struggle with the problem she named in 1866: people can fight for rights while narrowing whose rights count. Her work asks readers to notice who is missing from the platform, the ballot, the classroom, the archive, and the imagined future.

She also matters as a writer. Black literature was never only art after politics or politics dressed up as art. Harper's poems, stories, speeches, and novels show literature doing public work while still holding grief, faith, irony, tenderness, and argument.

A reader should leave Harper understanding that intersectional thinking has deep roots. She did not separate race from gender because her life would not let her. Her genius was making that truth speak in forms ordinary people could carry.
