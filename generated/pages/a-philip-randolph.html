May 1 · Labor

A. Philip Randolph

A. Philip Randolph understood something many powerful people hoped Black workers would never learn: a credible threat can be a form of power. Before the 1963 March on Washington became famous, Randolph had already used the idea of a mass march to pressure President Franklin Roosevelt into banning discrimination in wartime defense hiring. That victory did not come from a single speech. It came from decades of labor organizing, socialist journalism, union-building, and a refusal to separate civil rights from wages. Randolph made presidents bargain because he had helped Black workers build institutions strong enough to be taken seriously.

Full Story

A. Philip Randolph built civil-rights power through labor. That matters because the popular memory of the movement often starts with church pulpits, courtrooms, or lunch counters. Randolph's world included all of that, but it also included railroads, sleeping cars, union halls, wage theft, tips, long hours, and the daily humiliations Black workers endured while making white comfort possible.

Randolph was born in Florida in 1889 and moved to New York City in 1911. In Harlem he became a socialist, an editor, and a relentless critic of racial capitalism. With Chandler Owen, he published The Messenger, a magazine that challenged war, lynching, segregation, and exploitation. Federal officials watched him closely. Randolph was not asking simply for polite inclusion. He argued that Black freedom required economic power.

That belief shaped his work with Pullman porters. The Pullman Company employed thousands of Black men as sleeping car porters. The job could look respectable from the outside, but it was built on low wages, dependence on tips, exhausting hours, and racial servility. Passengers often called porters "George" after company founder George Pullman, erasing their names while demanding careful service. Organizing a union meant challenging one of the most powerful companies in the country.

In 1925, Randolph became president of the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters. The fight took years. The company spied on workers, fired organizers, and used fear to break momentum. Randolph and the Brotherhood persisted through losses, legal obstacles, and internal strain. In 1937, the Brotherhood won a contract with Pullman. The victory mattered beyond wages. It proved that Black workers could build a national labor institution and force a corporate giant to negotiate.

During World War II, Randolph turned that institutional power toward the federal government. Defense industries were hiring for war production, but Black workers were often shut out or trapped in the worst jobs. Randolph announced a March on Washington Movement and threatened to bring tens of thousands of Black Americans to the capital if Roosevelt did not act. The exact projected number shifted in public accounts, but the point was the same: the march was credible because Randolph had organizing roots.

The proposed march also changed the scale of Black protest politics. Randolph was not asking a handful of leaders to meet quietly with the president. He was imagining a mass demonstration in the capital before the 1963 march made that image familiar. That was risky. Some civil-rights leaders worried about confrontation. Some labor allies were cautious. Randolph knew that without visible pressure, Black workers would be asked to wait while defense dollars built white household wealth.

Roosevelt did not suddenly discover fairness. He faced political pressure. On June 25, 1941, he signed Executive Order 8802, banning discrimination in defense industries and creating the Fair Employment Practice Committee. The march was called off because the demand had produced federal action. Randolph had shown that Black protest could change presidential calculation before a crowd ever arrived.

He used a similar strategy around military segregation. Randolph threatened civil disobedience against a segregated draft and kept pressure on President Harry Truman. In 1948, Truman signed Executive Order 9981. Randolph later helped lead the 1963 March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom. The "Jobs" in that title was not decoration. It came from organizers who understood that voting, dignity, housing, and work were tied together.

Randolph's legacy is not only that he made presidents move. It is how he did it: by building durable worker institutions, connecting race and class, and treating Black workers as political actors rather than grateful recipients of reform. He made the federal government respond to organized Black labor.

Cost / Impact

Randolph paid for his politics with surveillance, hostility, and years of difficult organizing. The Pullman fight took more than a decade. Workers risked firings and retaliation. Randolph was repeatedly attacked as dangerous because he connected Black freedom to labor power and socialism.

The impact was enormous. The Brotherhood raised wages and recognition for Pullman porters. Executive Order 8802 opened wartime defense jobs to Black workers and created a federal enforcement mechanism. Executive Order 9981 helped dismantle formal military segregation. The 1963 march put jobs and freedom in the same sentence before the whole country.

Why It Matters Today

Randolph matters because civil rights history is incomplete when it forgets work. He understood that the right to vote mattered, but so did who got hired, who got promoted, who could join a union, and who controlled the terms of labor. His politics refused to treat economic exploitation as separate from racism.

His story also teaches strategy. Randolph did not rely on moral appeal alone. He built enough organized power that presidents had to count the cost of ignoring him. That is a different lesson from waiting for sympathetic leaders. It asks what institutions communities are building before the crisis arrives.

The 1963 March on Washington is often remembered through one speech. Randolph helps restore the full frame: the march was for jobs and freedom, and it grew from decades of labor organizing before it became a national television moment.
