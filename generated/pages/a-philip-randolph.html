Full Biography

The Pullman Palace Car Company ran sleeping cars on every major railroad in America, and for decades, every sleeping car porter on those trains was Black. It was one of the only stable jobs available to Black men after the Civil War. Porters worked brutally long hours, served wealthy white passengers, and were called "George" by customers who couldn't be bothered to learn their real names — a reference to George Pullman, the company's founder.

Asa Philip Randolph was born on April 15, 1889, in Crescent City, Florida, the son of a minister who instilled in him the belief that self-respect was non-negotiable. He moved to New York City in 1911, educated himself further, and became involved in socialist politics. In 1917, he and his colleague Chandler Owen co-founded The Messenger, a labor newspaper that called for Black workers to organize and resist. The federal government called it "the most dangerous magazine in America." The FBI watched Randolph for decades. He kept publishing.

In 1925, Pullman porters came to him. They wanted help organizing a union. The job looked stable from the outside but was built on exploitation: porters often worked over 400 hours a month, earned poverty wages, and had no legal protection against firing, harassment, or abuse. They depended on tips from passengers for basic income.

Randolph spent twelve years building the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters. The Pullman Company was the largest private employer of Black workers in America and had every advantage: money, political connections, and the legal right to fire any worker who organized. Randolph had none of that. What he had was organizing skill, a newspaper, and the refusal to quit.

In 1937, after twelve years, the Brotherhood won its first collective bargaining agreement — higher wages, capped hours, recognition as a union. It was the first such agreement ever won by a Black labor union in America. Randolph had turned a workforce of exploited porters into a political and economic force.

Then came World War II.

Defense factories were expanding rapidly, and the federal government was pouring money into wartime production. Black workers applied for jobs and were turned away. The discrimination was open and systemic. The NAACP filed complaints. The complaints went unanswered.

Randolph decided to force the issue another way.

He announced plans for a March on Washington — 100,000 Black Americans walking through the capital to demand an end to segregation in defense industries. He projected the number publicly and began building toward it. The march would embarrass a country claiming to defend democracy abroad while denying it at home.

President Roosevelt, who needed Black political support and could not afford the international optics of a mass protest while preparing to fight fascism, summoned Randolph to the White House. Roosevelt asked what he wanted. Randolph presented a draft executive order. The negotiations went back and forth. Randolph did not back down.

On June 25, 1941, Roosevelt signed Executive Order 8802, banning racial discrimination in hiring for wartime defense industries and federal agencies. The march was called off. The order established the Fair Employment Practice Committee to enforce the ban. It was the most significant federal action on civil rights since the Reconstruction era.

Seven years later, Randolph turned his attention to the military. He announced that he would counsel Black men to refuse military service unless the U.S. armed forces desegregated. In 1948, with a presidential election approaching, President Harry Truman signed Executive Order 9981, formally ending segregation in the military.

Randolph accomplished this twice — not by filing lawsuits, not by waiting for political goodwill, but by organizing enough power that refusal became more costly than compliance.

On August 28, 1963, Randolph stood at the podium at the Lincoln Memorial as the director of the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom. He was 74 years old. He had proposed the idea of this march in 1941, used its threat to win federal policy, and was now watching it finally happen at a scale he had always believed was possible. Martin Luther King Jr. delivered "I Have a Dream" on the same stage, after Randolph spoke.

A. Philip Randolph died on May 16, 1979, in New York City. He had built Black labor power, twice forced presidential executive action on civil rights, and helped design the most famous protest march in American history. He did it through decades of methodical organizing, not a single dramatic moment.

Wait... What?!

A. Philip Randolph threatened to march 100,000 Black workers on Washington in 1941 — and then called it off when Roosevelt agreed to desegregate defense industries.

The march never happened.

But it didn't need to. The threat was real because the union was real. Randolph had spent sixteen years building the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters into a genuine political force. Roosevelt knew he could actually do it.

Twenty-two years later, Randolph helped organize the actual March on Washington. He stood at the podium in 1963 at age 74. The idea he had been developing since 1941 finally happened on the largest scale in American history.

He had been building toward that day for over two decades.

Cost / Impact

Randolph spent twelve years fighting the Pullman Company — the largest private employer of Black workers in America — before winning the Brotherhood's first labor contract in 1937. The company had every advantage. Randolph kept organizing anyway.

The FBI surveilled him for decades. He was labeled a radical and called a communist for arguing that Black workers deserved the same wages and rights as white workers. During World War I, federal agents briefly arrested him for his antiwar views.

The impact was permanent. Executive Order 8802 opened wartime defense industries to Black workers who had been locked out. Executive Order 9981 ended segregation in the military — a change that affected millions of Black Americans in the decades that followed. The March on Washington, which Randolph helped design and lead, is part of the permanent record of American democratic protest.

He built the infrastructure that made each of these victories possible before any of them happened.

Freedom is never given; it is won.

— A. Philip Randolph

Why This Matters

Randolph demonstrated that organized power could force federal action even when the law was not on your side. His model — build real institutions, make credible demands, refuse to move — became the blueprint for modern civil rights strategy. The March on Washington of 1963, which defined the Civil Rights Movement for generations, was an idea he first proposed in 1941 and used as leverage before it ever took place.
