May 16 · Literature

Phillis Wheatley

Phillis Wheatley published a book before the United States existed, and powerful men still demanded proof that she had written it. Kidnapped from West Africa as a child, enslaved in Boston, educated in the Wheatley household, and published in London in 1773, she became the first Black woman in America to publish a book of poetry. That achievement is astonishing, but the deeper story is not only triumph. Wheatley wrote inside enslavement, patronage, Christian and classical learning, Revolutionary politics, and a white reading public that treated Black intelligence as something to be examined. Her page matters because her poetry exposed the lie of Black inferiority while showing how expensive recognition could be when freedom itself was still denied.

Full Story

Phillis Wheatley entered the historical record through violence. Around age seven, she was kidnapped from West Africa and forced across the Atlantic to Boston. The child who survived the voyage was purchased by the Wheatley family and named Phillis after the ship that carried her. That naming is a reminder that the archive often begins with what was done to her, not what she would have called herself before capture.

In the Wheatley household, Phillis learned English with unusual speed and was taught Latin, scripture, and classical literature. Her education was exceptional for an enslaved child in Boston, but exception should not be confused with freedom. The same household that encouraged her learning also held her in bondage. Her brilliance developed inside a system that made her personhood legally conditional.

Wheatley's poems circulated in newspapers and manuscript before her book appeared. She wrote elegies, religious poems, classical references, and occasional poems addressed to public figures. Her style belonged to the eighteenth-century Atlantic literary world. Modern readers sometimes want a more direct abolitionist voice from her, but that expectation can miss the constraints and conventions of her era. Wheatley wrote within forms that educated readers recognized, and her presence inside those forms was itself a challenge to racist assumptions.

Before her book could be published, a group of prominent Boston men examined her and signed an attestation that she had written the poems. The document is both evidence and insult. It helped make publication possible, but it also shows the racial disbelief surrounding her work. White authority demanded proof not because the poems lacked skill, but because the poet was an enslaved Black girl.

Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral was published in London in 1773, after Boston publishers did not bring it out. The book made Wheatley internationally known. She traveled to London, where abolitionist networks and literary patrons received her with interest. Soon after, she was manumitted. Yet freedom did not bring security.

The American Revolution changed the world around Wheatley and disrupted patronage networks. Members of the Wheatley family died. Phillis married John Peters, a free Black man, and struggled with poverty, illness, and the deaths of children. She tried to publish another volume but could not secure enough support. The poet who had been asked to prove her genius died young in Boston in 1784.

Wheatley's work has been argued over for generations. Some readers criticize her for using Christian and classical language that can seem restrained in the face of slavery. Others point to the subtlety of her critique, the difficulty of her position, and the radical fact of her authorship. Both the poems and the conditions of publication matter. She was writing to a world that doubted Black intellect and controlled Black bodies.

Her correspondence also shows a writer aware of the politics around her. Wheatley addressed George Washington, wrote elegies for public figures, and moved through networks where religion, abolition, empire, and revolution met. She was not outside the age of liberty talk. She was one of the people whose very presence exposed its contradictions. The colonies could declare rights while holding a poet in bondage and then ask whether she was capable of verse.

Phillis Wheatley matters because she makes the contradiction impossible to miss. A society that claimed liberty questioned whether an enslaved Black woman could think. Her book answered in polished couplets, biblical allusion, and Atlantic literary form. The answer did not free her from poverty or exploitation, but it left a record no racist theory could honestly survive.

Remember, Christians, Negroes, black as Cain, may be refin'd, and join th' angelic train.
Phillis Wheatley, "On Being Brought from Africa to America"
Cost / Impact

Wheatley's achievement cost more than talent. It required surviving kidnapping, enslavement, public examination, dependence on patrons, and a literary marketplace shaped by racism. Even after publication and manumission, she faced poverty and personal loss. Recognition did not become protection.

Her impact was immediate and lasting. Her book gave abolitionists evidence against claims of Black intellectual inferiority, while later Black writers inherited both her breakthrough and the burden of being measured against white doubts. Wheatley's career shows that publication can be liberation and constraint at the same time.

Why It Matters Today

Phillis Wheatley matters today because debates about Black authorship, proof, audience, and respectability have not disappeared. Her examination by Boston elites is an eighteenth-century version of a familiar demand: prove you belong before your work can be received.

Her story also asks readers to read historical texts on their own terms. Wheatley did not write like a twenty-first-century activist, and she should not have to. Her poetry worked inside the language available to her while making that language carry a Black woman's intellect across the Atlantic.

The fuller lesson is that firsts can be both glorious and lonely. Wheatley opened a door, but the world beyond it remained harsh. Her page should honor the brilliance without pretending recognition solved the conditions that made the brilliance so contested.
