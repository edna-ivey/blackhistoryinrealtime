August 15 · Politics

Maynard Jackson

Maynard Jackson did not treat representation as a portrait on the wall. In 1973, he was elected Atlanta's first Black mayor and one of the first Black mayors of a major Southern city. That victory mattered, but the larger story is what he did with power once he had it. Jackson insisted that the city's airport expansion, public contracts, and municipal decisions had to open doors for Black-owned and minority-owned businesses that had long been locked out. He was brilliant, forceful, sometimes combative, and willing to make Atlanta's business establishment uncomfortable. His page matters because it asks a harder question than who gets elected: what changes when a Black leader uses the budget, the bond market, the airport, and the procurement process as civil rights tools?

Full Story

Maynard Jackson came from a family that expected public life to be serious work. His maternal grandfather, John Wesley Dobbs, was a powerful Atlanta civic leader whose voter-registration work helped change Black political power in the city. Jackson was born in Dallas in 1938, moved through Atlanta's Black institutional world as a child, and graduated from Morehouse College while still a teenager. That background matters because he did not enter politics as a novelty. He entered it from a tradition of Black education, church leadership, law, and organized pressure.

In 1968, Jackson ran for the U.S. Senate against Herman Talmadge. He did not win statewide, but the race showed something important: Atlanta's Black electorate had become a force that could no longer be treated as symbolic. The next year, Jackson was elected vice mayor. In that role he learned the city's machinery from inside the building, and he also learned how hard the old power structure would fight to keep real control over money, land, policing, and development.

His 1973 mayoral campaign came at a turning point. Atlanta liked to call itself "the city too busy to hate," but slogans did not erase segregation, police violence, uneven investment, or exclusion from business opportunity. Jackson defeated incumbent Sam Massell and became Atlanta's first Black mayor. The victory was nationally visible, but Jackson's most consequential fight was not simply for a seat. It was for leverage.

The airport became the clearest test. Atlanta was expanding what would become one of the busiest airports in the world. Contractors, banks, law firms, engineers, and developers understood the project as a giant source of wealth. Jackson understood it that way too. He demanded meaningful participation for minority-owned businesses in airport contracts. Critics accused him of delaying progress or practicing reverse discrimination. Jackson answered with the obvious but rarely enforced truth: public money should not keep reproducing a private club.

That stance was not polite. Jackson could be blunt, and some contemporaries found him arrogant or heavy-handed. But the result changed Atlanta. Minority firms gained access to contracts, experience, bonding, and relationships that had been denied for generations. The airport expansion became a practical lesson in how a mayor could connect civil rights to procurement. A speech about equality mattered, but a contract could build a company.

Jackson also governed through the pressures that come with big-city leadership: labor tensions, policing, crime, federal funding limits, business resistance, and the daily demands of a changing metropolis. He did not solve every problem, and Atlanta's inequalities did not disappear under Black leadership. That is part of the lesson. Representation can open the door, but systems push back. Budgets, zoning, policing, schools, and private capital keep their own momentum unless leaders and communities keep forcing choices into daylight.

After two terms, Jackson left office, then returned for a third term in 1990. By then Atlanta was preparing for global attention through the 1996 Olympics, and the city had become a model for Black political leadership intertwined with business growth. That model brought opportunity and criticism. Atlanta became a center of Black middle-class power and municipal ambition, but also a city where displacement and unequal development remained unresolved.

Jackson's legacy is strongest when it is taught with the conflict still in it. He did not become historic only because he was first. He became historic because he used the powers of office to alter who got paid, who got hired, and who could build wealth from public growth. The airport bearing his name is not just a memorial. It is a reminder that infrastructure is political, and contracts can be a civil rights battlefield.

Cost / Impact

Jackson's choices cost him easy relationships with Atlanta's business establishment. He was accused of slowing projects, dividing the city, and putting race into spaces where race had already been operating quietly for decades. That backlash was part of the cost of making exclusion visible.

The impact was concrete. Minority-owned firms gained experience and capital through public contracting, especially around the airport expansion. The wider cost is that Atlanta's success story can become too glossy if it ignores displacement, inequality, and the limits of elite inclusion. Jackson expanded access to city power, but his story also shows that access has to be measured by material change, not applause.

Why It Matters Today

Maynard Jackson matters today because cities still spend enormous public money through contracts, incentives, infrastructure, policing, schools, and development deals. Those choices decide who builds wealth and who watches opportunity pass by. Jackson treated procurement as a civil rights arena before that language became common in equity offices and inclusion plans.

His story also complicates the idea that Black political power is only about symbolism. The first Black mayor of Atlanta changed the city's image, but his more durable work was administrative: percentages, bids, contract requirements, hiring, airport governance, and pressure on people who preferred custom over fairness.

The popular version says Atlanta elected a Black mayor and became a Black mecca. The fuller version asks who got to participate in that growth, who was left out, and what kind of courage it takes to turn a victory speech into a purchasing policy.
